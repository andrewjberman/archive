## THE PRINCIPLE

> **An investigation framed as "what went wrong" enumerates DEVIATIONS and ANOMALIES, and omits the actor who is
> ENTITLED to have done exactly this. The routine, authorised explanation is the one a fault-shaped question cannot see —
> and it is usually the correct one.**

**THE TEST, applied BEFORE the candidate list is written:**

> **Who is AUTHORISED to do this? Ask them FIRST.**
> Only then enumerate who might have done it without authorisation.

**Why it needs a test and not care:** the omission is produced by the QUESTION, not by inattention. Ask "who deviated?"
and a competent enumeration of deviations follows — complete, MECE within its own frame, and missing the legitimate
actor entirely. **The candidate set is not sloppy; it is precisely correct about the wrong population.** This is
Rule 24(i) collective-exhaustion failing against a DECLARED LAYER (Rule 24's 2026-08-20 scoping: *name the layer, then
hunt the uncovered member*) — and the uncovered member here is systematically the same one.

## THE SECOND-ORDER FAILURE, and it is the expensive one

**A conclusion drawn from a fault-framed set is stronger than the evidence supports, in a specific direction: it converts
UNASKED into UNACCOUNTED.** MSTR named this against themselves: *"With the authorized candidate unasked, I had no basis
for calling it unaccounted — I had a basis for calling it *unasked*. Those are different claims and I shipped the
stronger one."*

**UNASKED is a state of the investigation. UNACCOUNTED is a claim about the world.** The first is always true early; the
second is a finding. **A fault-framed enumeration makes the upgrade feel like a deduction** — every listed candidate was
genuinely eliminated, so the residue reads as anomaly rather than as the gap where the unlisted candidate sits.
