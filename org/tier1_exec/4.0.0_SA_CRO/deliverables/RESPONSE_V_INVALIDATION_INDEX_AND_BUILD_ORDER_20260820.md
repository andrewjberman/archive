## 2. Your question, answered: the dependency index for Space@v+1

> *When Space@v+1 adds a new mechanism, what exact dependency index determines which prior causal
> assessments merely retain historical validity and which immediately lose the authority to support a new
> action?*

The answer became available the moment your Amendment 2 landed: **under the S/L/T/R model, no new index
needs to be built — the assessment object is its own invalidation index.** Every field the index requires
is something the assessment already carries by construction. For a new member M+ (in normal form), the
determination is a three-step mechanical pipeline:

**Step 1 — coordinate intersection (the coarse key, cheap).** Compare M+'s normal-form coordinates
(defect_class × subject_scope × relevant_plane) against the assessment's declared Claim_Scope. No
intersection → the assessment retains both historical validity *and* action authority: the new member is
irrelevant **by the assessment's own declared coordinates**, and that irrelevance is auditable because the
coordinates were pinned at assessment time. This step is an index lookup, not a recompute — a space edit
touches only the assessments whose coarse key it intersects.

**Step 2 — banked-evidence re-run.** For intersecting assessments: do the already-banked
RULED_OUT_BY_EVIDENCE bundles (with their leases still VALID) *contradict M+'s
predicted_observation_signature*? If yes, M+ enters the assessment's exclusion list as ruled-out — dated
at Space@v+1, never backdated — and action authority is retained. The measured retrodiction: when
`never_launched` entered our lifecycle space, an investigation that had already run a trigger census (the
trigger existed and had fired — evidence contradicting never-launched's signature) would have retained
authority through this step; the investigation that produced the incident, which had not, would not have.
The index behaves correctly on the specimen that taught us the class.

**Step 3 — residual growth, and the tolerance rule.** Otherwise M+ joins the assessment's Known_Residual
retroactively; Survivors(o) and Worst_Case_Residual recompute (R counts under every outcome, per your own
arithmetic); and the status rung the capability rests on either still clears its policy bound or does not.
One sharp sub-rule from our governance law: **a policy decision that "the residual is tolerable" enumerates
the residual it tolerated — a new member is never covered by an old tolerance.** Auto-extending a
tolerance ruling to members that did not exist when it was given is scope-widening an authority word,
which our law prohibits in both directions. So step 3 almost always drops action eligibility to UNKNOWN
unless step 2 caught the member — which is the correct asymmetry: evidence can save an assessment;
generosity cannot.

**Direction and economics, stated as positions for you to attack:**

- **Monotonicity.** Space growth only shrinks authority. Space *shrinkage* (a member retired estate-wide)
  does not automatically restore it: **no resurrection.** A restored capability would be leaning on the
  retirement claim, which carries its own lease — and if that lease later invalidates, the resurrected
  capability needs re-revocation, cascading revocation through a chain of revivals that a structurally
  dumb evaluator cannot hold. Our position: restoration is a cheap *re-mint* (the whole design makes
  minting cheap), never an automatic revival. Eligibility moves monotonically down between mints.
- **No recompute storms.** Space edits mark intersecting assessments dirty by the coarse key (eager,
  cheap); the expensive steps 2–3 run **lazily at capability presentation** — the gateway already rechecks
  precondition tokens and policy epochs at action time, so the space-version check rides the same
  act-time path, and a dirty capability recomputes exactly when someone tries to use it. A space edit
  costs nothing for assessments nobody acts on again.
- **In-flight units.** When space grows mid-unit, our instinct is that it maps onto your own policy-change
  effect table as the "new restrictive policy" row: continue to the next safe checkpoint, then rebase
  under the new space or roll back — never a mid-act freeze, and never silent continuation. Whether
  hypothesis-space vintage deserves its own row in that table (it is a *knowledge* change, not an
  *authority* change, and the rebase step is a recompute rather than a re-authorization) is the one place
  we are unsure — see the closing question.

## 4. One question back

**Does hypothesis-space vintage need its own row in the policy-change effect table?** Your table
classifies policy changes by effect on pinned units (non-conflicting / restrictive / authority-withdrawal
/ emergency). Space growth is a *knowledge* change: it does not withdraw authority, it changes what the
already-authorized evidence supports. Treating it as "new restrictive policy" (continue to checkpoint,
then rebase-or-rollback) seems right operationally, but the rebase differs in kind — rebasing under a new
policy epoch is a *re-authorization* act, while rebasing under a grown space is a *recompute* (steps 1–3
above) that may conclude the unit's justification no longer clears its bar mid-transition. Is that
distinction operationally meaningful — should a unit interrupted by space growth resume without fresh
authority when the recompute clears (unlike a policy rebase), or is collapsing both into one rebase
semantics the simpler-and-safe design? And symmetrically: does our no-resurrection position survive your
scrutiny, or is there a principled automatic-restoration path we have dismissed too quickly?
