## 4. DOCUMENT-SPECIFIC FINDINGS

**4.1 — This is the highest-quality doctrine file in the corpus, and §9 is why.** It ends with **THE
CHECK**: seven questions an agent can run before recording a PASS. No other file converts its
incidents into a usable pre-flight. §9 is ~9 lines and carries most of the file's transferable value.

**4.2 — §7f is the clearest statement of M1 anywhere in the estate**, better than
CONTROLS_OVER_CARE's version: *"A GREEN SIGNAL DERIVED FROM A HARDCODED LIST IS NOT EVIDENCE OF
COVERAGE. IT IS EVIDENCE THAT THE LIST WAS ITERATED."* And §7f.3's **ORDERING RULE** (does the defect
mis-state an outcome or *change* one?) is the only severity-triage rule in the corpus. **Both are
buried at sub-sub-section depth in the third-largest file.**

**4.3 — §7f.1 is the single most counterintuitive finding in the whole corpus and it inverts a
security reflex:** tightening an IAM grant from wildcard to enumerated prefixes *accidentally*
created a completeness check; writing the *better* policy removed it. **"Tightening permissions does
not catch a forgotten subject."** Nothing else in the estate records a case where the correct
security posture destroyed a safety property.

**4.4 — §7e (a test asserting a prohibition is a ratchet against its own retraction) has no home.**
It is not about axes. It is about **records with teeth outliving their authority** — the same class
as the stale caveat (01/#55) and the retracted-but-still-asserted tuple (01/#46). Those three should
be one law and are in three files.
